Why we built it.

Most plans are either generic PDFs that assume every athlete starts in the same place, or an expensive coach. We wanted the middle: a plan built from the athlete's own training, counted back from their race, that keeps adjusting. We built it for our own ultra-trail block, tested it with Comrades marathoners, Ironman triathletes and ultra-trail runners, and now every training product we make runs on it.

Nine models, one engine.

What each part answers, how it works, the maths or rule at its core, and where the rule comes from. Exact weightings stay ours.

01

Athlete model

Where is this athlete right now?

Six ratings, each from the athlete's own data, rolled into one overall score: Endurance, Climbing, Aerobic engine, Durability, Recovery and Speed. Endurance uses six weeks of volume, the longest run in eight weeks and race history; Speed comes from PBs; Aerobic engine from metres per heartbeat and VO₂max; Recovery from sleep and HRV.

overall = Σ (rating × weight) · weights favour endurance and climbing for long events

Daniels, 2014 · Maunder et al., 2021

02

Pace model

How fast should each session be?

VDOT from the athlete's best recent result sets every training pace. Race times scale with Riegel's formula, and on trails each 100 m of climbing counts as an extra kilometre of effort, with pace adjusted for gradient.

VDOT = VO₂(v) ÷ %VO₂max(t) · T₂ = T₁ × (D₂ ÷ D₁)^1.06 · effort km = km + climb ÷ 100

Daniels, 2014 · Riegel, 1981 · Minetti et al., 2002

03

Load model

How hard has training really been?

Each session's load is its minutes in five heart-rate zones (set from lactate threshold heart rate), each zone weighted more heavily than the last, plus a charge for climbing. Rides, swims and gym work count at sport-specific rates and strength counts as minutes × effort (RPE). Daily loads feed a 7-day (acute) and 28-day (chronic) exponentially weighted average.

acuteₜ = loadₜ·α + acuteₜ₋₁·(1 − α), α = 2 ÷ (7 + 1) · chronic uses 28 days · ratio = acute ÷ chronic

Seiler, 2010 · Foster et al., 2001 · Gabbett, 2016 · Impellizzeri et al., 2020 · Soligard et al., 2016

04

Readiness model

Should today be hard, easy or off?

Nine morning signals are scored against the athlete's own normal, not a population average: HRV against a band from recent weekly averages, resting heart rate against the usual range, sleep, legs, niggles, stress, training load and easy-run efficiency. HRV and sleep carry the most weight. The result is a 0 to 100 score in five bands.

Primed ≥ 80 · Good ≥ 60 · Take care ≥ 40 · Go gently ≥ 20 · Back off < 20

Plews et al., 2013 · Kiviniemi et al., 2007 · Buchheit, 2014 · Halson, 2014 · Fullagar et al., 2015

05

Metres per heartbeat

Is aerobic fitness actually improving?

On flat, easy runs (low heart rate, at least 4 km, under 20 m of climbing per km), speed divided by heart rate gives the distance covered per heartbeat. The engine compares the average of the last five such runs with a month earlier. Run-to-run noise is about ±4%, so only bigger moves count as change.

m/beat = (distance in m ÷ moving seconds × 60) ÷ average heart rate

Our own measure, built and tested in Startline

06

Durability and climbing

Will the legs hold up late in the race?

Durability compares speed per heartbeat in the first and last third of every long run over 18 km: under 5% drift marks a durable athlete. The hill score combines four weeks of vertical metres, how often the athlete runs hills and their climb rate on uphill kilometres.

decoupling = (efficiencyfirst ⅓ − efficiencylast ⅓) ÷ efficiencyfirst ⅓ × 100

Maunder et al., 2021 · Smyth et al., 2022 · Bontemps et al., 2020

07

Plan model

What should the next weeks look like?

The block is counted back from the A race through base, build, peak and taper, with B and C races used as training. Weekly volume builds in steps from what the athlete already does, with lighter weeks, two strength sessions a week through the build, and sessions placed at the time of day that suits them.

taper ≈ 2 weeks, volume down 41–60%, intensity kept · strength 2× a week · most minutes easy

Bosquet et al., 2007 · Mujika and Padilla, 2003 · Seiler and Kjerland, 2006 · Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007 · Helgerud et al., 2007 · Blagrove et al., 2018 · Chtourou and Souissi, 2012

08

Race model

What happens on the day?

A predicted time with a range, course-based pacing by gradient, a fuel plan that turns grams of carbohydrate per hour into which product to take and when, carb loading for races over 90 minutes, heat preparation and a race-week countdown.

fuel: g/h × interval ÷ 60 per stop · carb load only for races > 90 min

Jeukendrup, 2014 · Jeukendrup, 2017 · Burke et al., 2011 · Impey et al., 2018 · Périard et al., 2015

09

Consistency model

Is the athlete doing the basics every week?

Five weekly habits are tracked and rewarded: five morning check-ins, two strength sessions, at least 90% of the planned kilometres, easy runs that stay under the heart-rate cap, and three nights of eight hours. Being brilliant with the basics beats any single heroic session.

week done = check-ins ≥ 5 · strength ≥ 2 · km ≥ 0.9 × plan · easy HR ≤ cap · nights ≥ 8 h × 3

Afremow, 2014 · Halson, 2014 · Milewski et al., 2014

How a plan is built.

Six steps, the same every time, so a plan can always be explained.

01

The questionnaire

Race, date and distance; current weekly volume and longest recent session; days and hours available; experience, niggles and goal time. One short form, step by step.

02

Counted back from race day

The block is laid out backwards: race week, taper, peak, build and base, with A, B and C races placed so the taper goes where it matters.

03

Load that builds safely

Weekly volume rises in steps from what the athlete already does, with a lighter week every few weeks, and no single long session jumps far past the longest one of the last month.

04

Easy mostly, hard on purpose

Most sessions are easy and a few are hard, with paces from a recent race (VDOT) or heart-rate zones, so the hard days are hard enough to count.

05

It adapts as sessions come in

Completed sessions feed training load, form and readiness, and the coming week adjusts when an athlete is tired, sick or ahead of schedule.

06

Out the other end

A plan in the app, a branded PDF to download, and the session targets for every day until the start line.

    Guardrails.

    Rules that sit above the plan and protect the athlete, whatever the plan says.

    • Weekly jumpsA week more than about 30% above the recent norm is flagged and the rest of the week is kept easy. Nielsen et al., 2014
    • Single long sessionsNo planned long run jumps far past the longest one of the previous 30 days. Frandsen et al., 2025
    • Load spikesAcute load above 1.3× the chronic norm is "high" and above 1.5× is "spiking": the engine eases off, and treats the ratio as a warning light, not a prediction. Gabbett, 2016
    • Bad morningsA readiness score in "Go gently" or "Back off" swaps a hard session for an easy one or rest, and says why. Plews et al., 2013
    • Easy means easyEasy runs carry a heart-rate cap, and the weekly intensity mix is checked against target bands for low aerobic, high aerobic and anaerobic work. Seiler, 2010
    • Under-fuellingMissed periods, heavy fatigue and low energy are flagged with a prompt to see a doctor, never a diagnosis. Mountjoy et al., 2018
    • HeatHot races get a heat plan: 1 to 2 weeks of heat sessions, or a hot bath after easy runs. Zurawlew et al., 2016
    • Not medical adviceEvery plan carries a health disclaimer and an indemnity before purchase. Pain, chest symptoms and illness always override the plan.

    The research behind it.

    Rules come from published sports science, not from a chatbot. The headline ideas first, then every source the engine uses.

    Paces from performance

    VDOT training paces from Jack Daniels' running formula, so every session has a target pace set from a real recent result.

    About 80% easy

    Endurance athletes in most studied sports do most of their training at low intensity, a pattern described by Stephen Seiler. The engine keeps hard days rare and deliberate.

    A taper that keeps intensity

    A meta-analysis of tapering (Bosquet and colleagues, 2007) found the best results from about two weeks with volume cut by roughly 41 to 60% while intensity stays. That is the default taper.

    No big single-session jumps

    A 2025 study of more than 5,000 runners (Frandsen and colleagues, British Journal of Sports Medicine) linked a single run more than 10% longer than the longest of the previous 30 days to a higher injury risk. The engine caps long sessions with that in mind.

    Fitness and fatigue

    Training load, fitness and form follow the impulse-response model first described by Banister, so a block shows whether it is building the athlete or digging a hole.

    Our own measures

    Metres per heartbeat (grade-adjusted speed per heartbeat on easy runs) and readiness against the athlete's own normal, both built and tested in Startline.

    From our founder's bookshelf.

    The studies set the numbers. The books shaped how we think about training. These are notes our founder took while reading, and where each idea now lives in the engine.

    From the notes

    Outlive

    Peter Attia, 2023

    Zone 2 sits around 70 to 85% of threshold power; once a week, four minutes as hard as you can hold with four easy minutes between, four to six times. Carry heavy things, train grip, load muscles on the way down as well as up. Protein of 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg a day. Sleep of 7 to 8.5 hours is non-negotiable.

    In the engineAn easy base with hard work kept to a few deliberate sessions, eccentric quad strength in every build, protein at every meal and 20 to 30 g after key sessions, and sleep as one of the heaviest readiness signals.

    From the notes

    The Champion's Mind

    Jim Afremow, 2014

    Compile a personal scouting report on your abilities. Win the day. Be brilliant with the basics: the training plan, the eating plan, regular sleep, time to relax. Compete against yourself, not others. Fill the tank with good racing fuel and never slide into disordered eating.

    In the engineThe athlete card is that scouting report; the weekly habits reward the basics; every comparison is against the athlete's own normal; and under-fuelling raises a flag, never a target.

    From the notes

    Huberman Lab with Dr Andy Galpin

    Podcast series, 2023

    Construct a plan that lives within your realistic limits. Volume up about 5% a week and never more than 10%. Build for around six weeks, then a lighter week. Consistency beats intensity. Gear 1 is nasal breathing: if you can breathe through your nose, you are easy. Athletes should train at the time they will compete.

    In the engineThe questionnaire starts from days and hours actually available; the plan model caps weekly growth and adds lighter weeks; easy days are policed; and long sessions line up with race start times.

    From the notes

    Mitochondrial biogenesis

    Our founder's study notes

    Endurance work at easy intensity drives the signals (AMPK and PGC-1α) that build new mitochondria, which is why the base matters. High-dose antioxidants straight after training can blunt that adaptation. Fuel the hard sessions properly; some easy sessions can be done with less.

    In the engineWhy most minutes stay easy, why the supplement guide is graded and cautious, and why fuelling is matched to the session rather than one rule for every day.

    From the shelf

    80/20 Running

    Matt Fitzgerald, 2014

    About 80% of training at low intensity and 20% at moderate to high intensity, with most recreational runners doing their easy runs too hard.

    In the engineThe intensity mix is checked against target bands every four weeks, and easy runs carry a heart-rate cap.

    Matthew Walker, 2017

    Sleep is the foundation that recovery, learning and injury resistance are built on.

    In the engineSleep feeds the readiness score, three nights of eight hours are a weekly habit, and a short night lowers readiness, which can turn a hard day into an easy one.

    Questions.

    Is the Startline Engine AI?

    No. Plans are built by written rules and algorithms, so the same answers give the same plan and every rule can be checked against the research. Our Startline app adds an optional AI coach on top, but the engine itself does not depend on it.

    Which sports does it cover?

    Road and trail running from 5 km to ultra distance, triathlon and HYROX.

    How is this different from a free plan off the internet?

    A free plan assumes everyone starts in the same place. The engine starts from the athlete's own volume, longest recent session, available days and race date, and keeps adjusting once training starts.

    Is it medical advice?

    No. Plans are general training guidance based on what the athlete tells us. Every plan carries a health disclaimer, and athletes are asked to see a doctor before starting if they have any doubts.
